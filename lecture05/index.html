Density Functional Theory
The Many-Body Problem & Hohenberg-Kohn Theorems

Real molecules have many electrons. The exact wave function lives in impossibly high dimensions. DFT replaces it with a 3D electron density — and nothing is lost.

Motivation · the challenge

From one particle to many electrons

What we've solved so far

  • Particle in a box — 1 particle, exact solution
  • Quantum harmonic oscillator — 1 particle, exact solution
  • Hydrogen atom — 1 electron + 1 nucleus, exact solution

All of these are single-particle problems. The Schrödinger equation separates cleanly, and we get analytical solutions.

The real world

  • Anthracene (蒽): 94 electrons
  • C₆₀ fullerene: 360 electrons
  • P3HT monomer: 100+ electrons
  • A protein: thousands of electrons

These many-electron problems cannot be solved exactly. We need a fundamentally new approach.

Central question

How can we compute the electronic structure of a real molecule — orbital energies, geometry, spectra — from first principles, without experimental parameters?

The exact equation

The many-body Schrödinger equation

Full Hamiltonian: five terms

For $M$ nuclei and $N$ electrons:

$\hat{H}_{\text{total}} = \hat{T}_N + \hat{T}_e + \hat{V}_{NN} + \hat{V}_{eN} + \hat{V}_{ee}$
TermPhysics
$\hat{T}_N$Nuclear kinetic energy
$\hat{T}_e = -\sum_i \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla_i^2$Electron kinetic energy
$\hat{V}_{NN}$Nucleus–nucleus repulsion
$\hat{V}_{eN} = -\sum_{i,A} \frac{Z_A e^2}{|\vec{r}_i - \vec{R}_A|}$Electron–nucleus attraction
$\hat{V}_{ee} = \sum_{iElectron–electron repulsion

The wave function

$\Psi(\vec{r}_1, \vec{r}_2, \ldots, \vec{r}_N; \vec{R}_1, \ldots, \vec{R}_M)$

This is a function of $3(N+M)$ coordinates — the positions of every electron and every nucleus.

The villain

$\hat{V}_{ee}$ couples every electron to every other electron. The position of electron $i$ depends on where all other electrons are. This makes the equation inseparable — no analytical solution exists for $N > 1$.

Compare with hydrogen

For H atom: $\hat{V}_{ee} = 0$ (only one electron). The equation separates into $r$, $\theta$, $\phi$ parts → exact solution. Add one more electron (He) and exact solution is already impossible.

The fundamental barrier

The dimensionality catastrophe

How much memory to store $\Psi$?

$\Psi(\vec{r}_1, \ldots, \vec{r}_N)$ lives in $3N$-dimensional space.

  • Use 10 grid points per coordinate
  • Total grid points: $10^{3N}$
  • Each point needs 8 bytes (double precision)
  • Storage needed: $8 \times 10^{3N}$ bytes

Atoms in the observable universe: $\sim 10^{80}$

Conclusion

For any molecule larger than a few electrons, directly storing $\Psi$ is physically impossible — not because computers aren't fast enough, but because the universe is too small.

First simplification

Born-Oppenheimer approximation: freeze the nuclei

The mass gap

Proton mass / electron mass = 1836

Nuclei move $\sim\sqrt{1836} \approx 43$ times slower than electrons. From the electron's perspective, nuclei are essentially frozen in place.

The approximation

Treat nuclear positions $\{\vec{R}_A\}$ as fixed parameters, not variables. Solve only for the electrons:

$\hat{H}_e\,\Psi_e = E_e\,\Psi_e$
$\hat{H}_e = \hat{T}_e + \hat{V}_{eN} + \hat{V}_{ee}$

$\hat{V}_{NN}$ is just a constant (nuclei are fixed) — add it back later.

Two-step strategy

Step 1: Fix nuclear positions → solve electron problem → get energy $E_e(\{\vec{R}\})$.
Step 2: Vary nuclear positions → map out the potential energy surface → study nuclear motion on this surface.

Still not enough

Dimensions reduced from $3(N+M)$ to $3N$, but $3N$ is still astronomical. For anthracene: $3 \times 94 = 282$ dimensions. We need a fundamentally different approach.

Why DFT wins

DFT achieves accuracy close to CCSD(T) but with computational cost similar to Hartree-Fock. This makes it the most widely used quantum chemistry method for organic electronics.

The key insight

All other methods try to approximate the $3N$-dimensional wave function $\Psi$. DFT asks a radical question: can we bypass $\Psi$ entirely and work with the 3D electron density $\rho(\vec{r})$ instead?

The central quantity

Electron density: from $3N$ dimensions to 3

Definition

$\rho(\vec{r}) = N\!\int |\Psi(\vec{r}, \vec{r}_2, \ldots, \vec{r}_N)|^2 \, d\vec{r}_2 \cdots d\vec{r}_N$

Physical meaning: $\rho(\vec{r})\,d^3r$ = probability of finding any one electron in the volume element $d^3r$ at position $\vec{r}$.

Key properties

  • $\rho(\vec{r}) \geq 0$ — it's a probability density, always non-negative
  • $\int \rho(\vec{r})\,d^3r = N$ — integrates to total number of electrons
  • $\rho(\vec{r})$ is a function of ordinary 3D space — regardless of how many electrons the system has
Experimentally observable

$\rho(\vec{r})$ is what you measure in X-ray diffraction experiments. It is a real, physical observable — unlike the abstract $3N$-dimensional wave function.

The bold question

Going from $\Psi$ ($3N$ dimensions) to $\rho$ (3 dimensions) is a massive compression. Surely we lose information? Hohenberg and Kohn proved that, for the ground state, we lose nothing.

Hohenberg-Kohn, 1964

First theorem: density determines everything

Theorem statement

For a system of $N$ electrons in an external potential $V_{\text{ext}}(\vec{r})$ with a non-degenerate ground state, the ground-state electron density $\rho_0(\vec{r})$ uniquely determines $V_{\text{ext}}(\vec{r})$ (up to an additive constant).

The logical chain

$\rho_0 \;\to\; V_{\text{ext}} \;\to\; \hat{H} \;\to\; \Psi_0 \;\to\; \text{everything}$

Once you know $\rho_0$, you can (in principle) reconstruct the external potential, the Hamiltonian, the wave function, and all ground-state properties: energy, forces, dipole moment, polarizability…

Why this is astonishing

  • From $\Psi$ to $\rho$ is a colossal compression: $3N$ dimensions → 3 dimensions
  • Common sense says: compressing so much must lose information
  • HK theorem says: for the ground state, no information is lost
  • A 3D function contains exactly the same physics as the $3N$-dimensional wave function
Contrapositive

Two different external potentials (differing by more than a constant) cannot produce the same ground-state density. The mapping $V_{\text{ext}} \to \rho_0$ is injective (one-to-one).

The proof · part I

Proof by contradiction: setting up two systems

This proof is one of the most elegant in quantum mechanics — short, beautiful, and purely based on the variational principle.

Step 1 — Assume the opposite

Suppose two different external potentials $V_1$ and $V_2$ (with $V_1 - V_2 \neq$ const) give the same ground-state density $\rho_0$.

They define two Hamiltonians with different ground states:

$\hat{H}_1\Psi_1 = E_1\Psi_1, \quad \hat{H}_2\Psi_2 = E_2\Psi_2$

Since $V_1 \neq V_2 + \text{const}$, we must have $\Psi_1 \neq \Psi_2$.

Step 2 — Apply variational principle to System 1

$\Psi_1$ is the ground state of $\hat{H}_1$, so for any other state (including $\Psi_2$):

$E_1 < \langle\Psi_2|\hat{H}_1|\Psi_2\rangle$

Write $\hat{H}_1 = \hat{H}_2 + \sum_i[V_1(\vec{r}_i) - V_2(\vec{r}_i)]$:

$E_1 < E_2 + \int [V_1 - V_2]\,\rho_0\,d^3r \quad \text{(A)}$

We used: $\langle\Psi_2|\hat{H}_2|\Psi_2\rangle = E_2$ and the fact that single-body operator expectations can be written as integrals over $\rho$.

The proof · part II

The contradiction: $0 < 0$

Step 3 — Apply variational principle to System 2

By perfect symmetry (swap labels 1 ↔ 2):

$E_2 < E_1 + \int [V_2 - V_1]\,\rho_0\,d^3r \quad \text{(B)}$

Step 4 — Add (A) + (B)

$E_1 + E_2 < E_2 + E_1 + \underbrace{\int[V_1 - V_2]\rho_0\,d^3r + \int[V_2 - V_1]\rho_0\,d^3r}_{= 0}$

The two integral terms cancel exactly, giving:

$E_1 + E_2 < E_1 + E_2$
$0 < 0 \quad$ Contradiction! $\;\blacksquare$
Therefore

Our assumption was wrong. Two different external potentials cannot give the same ground-state density. The mapping $\rho_0 \to V_{\text{ext}}$ is unique. QED.

Beauty of the proof

Only two ingredients: the variational principle (from Lecture 03) and the density-expectation value relationship for single-body operators. No approximations anywhere — this is an exact theorem.

Implications

What HK-I really means: density is the fundamental variable

The paradigm shift

  • Before HK: the wave function $\Psi$ is the fundamental object. $\rho$ is just a derived quantity.
  • After HK: $\rho_0$ contains all ground-state information. $\Psi$ is, in principle, redundant.

Every ground-state property $O$ is a functional of the density:

$O = O[\rho_0]$

Energy, dipole moment, polarizability, forces on nuclei — all of them.

Important caveat

HK-I says these functionals exist, but doesn't tell us their explicit form. It's an existence theorem, not a construction manual.

Analogy

Imagine compressing a movie (terabytes) into a single photograph. You'd expect to lose most of the content. HK-I says: for the "ground-state movie" of electrons, the photograph is lossless. The 3D density snapshot encodes the entire $3N$-dimensional story.

Hohenberg-Kohn, 1964

Second theorem: the variational principle for density

Theorem statement

There exists a universal energy functional $E[\rho]$ such that, for the correct $V_{\text{ext}}$, the true ground-state density $\rho_0$ minimizes $E[\rho]$ among all trial densities satisfying $\rho \geq 0$ and $\int\rho\,d^3r = N$:

$E_0 = E[\rho_0] \leq E[\tilde\rho]$

What this gives us

An operational strategy: search over all possible 3D densities, and the one that minimizes $E[\rho]$ is the true ground-state density.

This is the density analog of the variational principle for wave functions — but now we vary over 3D functions instead of $3N$-dimensional ones!

Structure of the energy functional

$E[\rho] = T[\rho] + V_{eN}[\rho] + V_{ee}[\rho]$
TermMeaningKnown?
$V_{eN}[\rho] = \int V_{\text{ext}}\rho\,d^3r$External potential energyYes, exactly
$T[\rho]$Kinetic energyNo!
$V_{ee}[\rho]$Electron–electron interactionNo!
The gap

HK-II proves the variational principle works, but we don't know the explicit form of $T[\rho]$ and $V_{ee}[\rho]$. Early attempts (Thomas-Fermi model) failed badly — they couldn't even predict chemical bonding!

Energy functional

What we know, what we don't, and why it matters

The universal functional $F[\rho]$

Separate the known external potential part from the unknown "internal" part:

$E[\rho] = \underbrace{\int V_{\text{ext}}\rho\,d^3r}_{\text{known}} + \underbrace{F[\rho]}_{\text{universal, unknown}}$

$F[\rho] = T[\rho] + V_{ee}[\rho]$ is universal: it's the same functional for every electronic system — hydrogen, anthracene, DNA. Only $V_{\text{ext}}$ changes from system to system.

Why "universal" matters

If someone discovers the exact $F[\rho]$, it would solve all of chemistry and materials science in one stroke. Every molecule, every solid, every surface — the same functional.

The Thomas-Fermi failure

The earliest attempt at a kinetic energy functional:

$T_{\text{TF}}[\rho] = C_F\int\rho^{5/3}\,d^3r$

This assumes locally uniform density — fatal for molecules where density varies sharply near nuclei and in bonds. Result: no chemical bonding predicted at all.

Fine print

Limitations of the Hohenberg-Kohn theorems

1. Ground state only

HK theorems guarantee uniqueness and variational principle only for the ground state. Excited states require a different framework → TD-DFT (Runge-Gross theorem, a later lecture).

2. Functional form unknown

The theorems prove that $E[\rho]$ exists, but give no recipe for constructing it. This is the central unsolved problem of DFT — the entire field of functional development is dedicated to finding good approximations.

3. Non-degenerate ground state

The proof uses the strict inequality from the variational principle, which requires a non-degenerate ground state. Degenerate cases need extensions (Levy constrained search formulation, 1979).

4. $v$-representability

Not every function satisfying $\rho \geq 0, \int\rho = N$ corresponds to the ground state of some external potential. The variational search may visit "unphysical" densities.

Bottom line

HK provides the theoretical foundation, but we need a practical scheme to actually compute anything. This is where Kohn and Sham enter — next lecture.

Summary

DFT Foundations · Cheat Sheet

Core ideas

  • Many-body problem: $\hat{V}_{ee}$ couples all electrons → no exact solution for $N > 1$
  • Dimensionality catastrophe: $\Psi$ lives in $3N$ dimensions → cannot be stored or computed directly
  • Born-Oppenheimer: separate fast electrons from slow nuclei → still $3N$ dimensions
  • HK-I: $\rho_0(\vec{r})$ uniquely determines $V_{\text{ext}}$ → 3D density contains all ground-state physics
  • HK-II: variational principle works for density → minimize $E[\rho]$ to find ground state
$\rho_0 \;\to\; V_{\text{ext}} \;\to\; \hat{H} \;\to\; \Psi_0 \;\to\; \text{everything}$

Key equations

$\rho(\vec{r}) = N\int|\Psi(\vec{r},\vec{r}_2,\ldots)|^2\,d\vec{r}_2\cdots$
$E[\rho] = T[\rho] + \int V_{\text{ext}}\rho\,d^3r + V_{ee}[\rho]$
$E_0 = \min_{\rho} E[\rho]$
The open problem

HK theorems prove the density is enough, but don't give us the functional $F[\rho] = T[\rho] + V_{ee}[\rho]$. Direct approximations (Thomas-Fermi) fail catastrophically.
