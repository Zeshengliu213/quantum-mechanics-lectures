The Infinite Square Well

Why does increasing conjugation usually make the band gap smaller?

Motivation · organic electronics

Why does a more conjugated molecule have a smaller band gap?

The chemical observation

As a $\pi$ system becomes more conjugated, its electronic absorption usually shifts to longer wavelength. In molecular-orbital language, the HOMO-LUMO gap becomes smaller.

The model answer we want

A longer conjugated segment lets the $\pi$ electron wave spread over a larger distance. Larger spatial extent means more closely spaced standing waves.

larger conjugation length $L$ → smaller level spacing

The infinite square well is not a precise molecular theory. It is a clean way to understand this trend qualitatively.

Question to pose

If an electron wave has more room to spread out, should adjacent allowed energies move closer together or farther apart?

Physical setup

Trapping an electron in a 1D box

The model

An electron is confined to a one-dimensional interval of width $L$. Inside the box the potential is zero (the electron moves freely); at the walls the potential is infinitely high (the electron can never escape).

Where do the boundary conditions come from?

The wave function $\psi(x)$ must be continuous everywhere. Outside the walls the potential is infinite → the electron cannot be there → $\psi=0$ there.

Continuity then forces the inside $\psi$ to match the outside at the edges:

$\psi(0)=0,\qquad \psi(L)=0$

These two unassuming conditions are the source of every quantization result that follows.

Why this model matters

It is simple enough to solve completely by hand, yet it already contains the three core features of quantum mechanics: energy quantization, zero-point energy, and wave-function nodes. It is the first piece of the puzzle for every more complex system.

Solving it · main line

From the Schrödinger equation to discrete energy levels

1

Start from the time-independent Schrödinger equation

$\left[-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{d^2}{dx^2}+V(x)\right]\psi(x)=E\psi(x)$

This says kinetic energy plus potential energy acting on $\psi$ gives the allowed total energy $E$.

2

Use the inside-the-box potential

$0<x<L:\quad V(x)=0$
$-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x)=E\psi(x)$

Inside the well the electron is free, so only the curvature term remains.

3

Rearrange into a standing-wave equation

$\psi''(x)=-\dfrac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\psi(x)$
$k^2\equiv\dfrac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \psi''(x)=-k^2\psi(x)$

The unknown energy has been packaged into the wave number $k$.

4

Solve the differential equation

$r^2=-k^2\quad\Rightarrow\quad r=\pm ik$
$\psi(x)=A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx)$

The allowed shape must be a mixture of sine and cosine waves.

5

Apply the left boundary condition

$\psi(0)=A\sin0+B\cos0=B=0$
$\Rightarrow\quad \psi(x)=A\sin(kx)$

The cosine piece is removed because the wave function must vanish at $x=0$.

6

Apply the right boundary condition

$\psi(L)=A\sin(kL)=0$
$A\neq0\quad\Rightarrow\quad \sin(kL)=0$

$A=0$ would mean no particle at all, so the sine factor must be zero.

7

Turn the boundary condition into allowed wave numbers

$\sin(kL)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad kL=n\pi$
$k_n=\dfrac{n\pi}{L},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$

$n=0$ gives $\psi\equiv0$; negative $n$ only changes the overall sign, so the physical states start at $1$.

8

Convert allowed wave numbers into allowed energies

$k^2=\dfrac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad E=\dfrac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}$
$E_n=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\dfrac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2=\dfrac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$

The energy becomes discrete because $k$ became discrete.

9

Write the same energy using $h$ instead of $\hbar$

$\hbar=\dfrac{h}{2\pi}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \pi^2\hbar^2=\dfrac{h^2}{4}$
$E_n=\dfrac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}$

These two forms are identical; use whichever constant is more convenient.

10

Normalize the wave function

$\int_0^L|\psi_n(x)|^2dx=1$
$|A|^2\int_0^L\sin^2\!\dfrac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx=|A|^2\dfrac{L}{2}=1$
$\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{L}}\sin\!\dfrac{n\pi x}{L}$

The coefficient is fixed by requiring total probability inside the box to be $1$.

Infinite square well

Final result

$E_n=\dfrac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}=\dfrac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$
$\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{L}}\sin\dfrac{n\pi x}{L}$
Presentation cue

Use one click per algebra move: equation → substitution → boundary condition → quantized $k$ → quantized $E$ → normalized $\psi_n$.

Core idea

The walls force $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$. That single constraint filters a continuous wave number $k$ into $k_n=n\pi/L$.

Concept checkpoint

Stop after the derivation: what exactly did we prove?

Where did discreteness enter the argument?

At the boundary condition $\psi(L)=0$. The equation itself allows any $k$, but $\sin(kL)=0$ only allows $kL=n\pi$.

Why is $n=0$ rejected?

If $n=0$, then $k=0$ and $\psi(x)=A\sin0=0$ everywhere. That describes no particle, not a valid state.

Why do negative $n$ values not create new states?

$\sin(-n\pi x/L)=-\sin(n\pi x/L)$. Multiplying a wave function by $-1$ does not change $|\psi|^2$, so it is the same physical state.

Why does $E_n$ grow like $n^2$?

The kinetic-energy operator measures curvature. Higher $n$ means shorter wavelength, larger $k$, and $E=\hbar^2k^2/2m$.

Interpretation

What does the wave function actually tell us?

$\psi(x)$ is an amplitude, not a probability

The wave function can be positive, negative, or complex. Its sign matters for interference, but a sign by itself is not a probability.

$\text{probability density}=|\psi(x)|^2$

Probability lives in intervals

The probability of finding the electron between $a$ and $b$ is the area under $|\psi|^2$:

$\displaystyle P(a<x<b)=\int_a^b|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$

A point has zero width, so we speak about probability density at a point, not probability at a point.

Nodes are forbidden positions

At a node, $\psi=0$, so $|\psi|^2=0$. A measurement will never find the electron exactly at that interior node.

  • $n=1$: no interior nodes
  • $n=2$: one interior node at $x=L/2$
  • $n=3$: two interior nodes at $x=L/3$ and $2L/3$
Important warning

The electron is not sliding along the sine curve. The curve tells us the spatial structure of the quantum state, not a classical path.

Three key physical features

What this simple model teaches us

Feature 1 · Quantization

Energy takes only discrete values

Discreteness is not an assumption imposed by hand. It is the mathematical consequence of "the wave function must vanish at the walls" — only standing waves of particular wavelengths fit in the box.

Like a guitar string fixed at both ends — only certain notes are allowed.

Feature 2 · Zero-point energy

$E_1 \neq 0$ — the electron can't stop

The lowest energy $E_1 = h^2/8mL^2 > 0$. The electron can never sit perfectly still at the bottom of the box.

This agrees with the uncertainty principle: confining the electron to width $L$ ($\Delta x\sim L$) forces momentum fluctuations ($\Delta p\gtrsim\hbar/L$), so the kinetic energy $\sim(\Delta p)^2/2m>0$.

Feature 3 · Nodes

Wave-function shape and node count

$\psi_n$ has $n-1$ nodes (interior points where the wave function is zero).

More nodes → the wave function bends harder inside the box → larger curvature → larger kinetic energy. This is the physical origin of "more nodes, higher energy".

Physical meaning · zero-point energy

Why the ground state still has kinetic energy

Route 1: from the exact solution

$E_1=\dfrac{h^2}{8mL^2}>0$

The lowest allowed state is not a flat line. It is a half sine wave, so it has curvature. Curvature costs kinetic energy.

$\hat T=-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{d^2}{dx^2}$

Route 2: from uncertainty

$\Delta x\sim L$
$\Delta p\gtrsim\dfrac{\hbar}{L}$
$E_{\min}\sim\dfrac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}\sim\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$

The scaling matches the exact answer: stronger confinement raises the minimum kinetic energy.

Classroom contrast

Classically, the particle could sit still with $E=0$. Quantum mechanically, a completely still particle would have definite momentum and definite position range at the same time, which is not allowed.

Back to organic electronics

Conjugation length controls the band gap qualitatively

π electrons ≈ particle in a box

The delocalized $\pi$ electrons of a conjugated molecule can be approximated as particles trapped in a 1D "box". The effective box length is the conjugation length:

$L \approx (N_C+1)\,d,\quad d\approx 1.4\,\text{Å}$

$N_C$ is the number of conjugated carbon atoms, $d$ the average C–C bond length.

The chain of reasoning

Longer chain → larger $L$ → since $E_n\propto 1/L^2$, the HOMO–LUMO gap $\Delta E$ shrinks → absorbed photon energy is lower → wavelength $\lambda\propto L^2$ is longer → absorption redshifts.

Qualitative trend

The infinite-well model gives the direction of the effect: more delocalization means smaller spacing between allowed electronic states.

Limits of the model

The free-electron model is for intuition, not accurate prediction. Real gaps also depend on bond-length alternation, electron–electron repulsion, substituents, geometry, and solid-state environment.

Extension · a more realistic box

The finite square well — when the walls aren't infinitely high

Why we need it

In real molecules the barrier confining an electron is of finite height. The electron is bound, but not absolutely forbidden from leaving — the infinite well is only an idealized first approximation.

Four key changes

  • The wave function penetrates the barrier, decaying exponentially $\psi\propto e^{-\kappa x}$ outside; it no longer vanishes exactly at the walls
  • The number of bound states is finite (no longer infinite)
  • All energy levels shift down; the wave function is more spread out than in the infinite well
  • Effective box length > actual well width → end effects matter more for short conjugated chains
Tunneling preview

That the wave function can "leak" into the classically forbidden region is the prelude to quantum tunneling — Lecture 5 covers how charge crosses the barrier at metal/organic interfaces this way.

Extension · universality of the model

From 1D to 3D — particle in a box and quantum dots

The 3D well

Confine the particle in a three-dimensional box and the energy is set by three quantum numbers $(n_x,n_y,n_z)$:

$E=\dfrac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m}\!\left(\dfrac{n_x^2}{L_x^2}+\dfrac{n_y^2}{L_y^2}+\dfrac{n_z^2}{L_z^2}\right)$

Each direction is its own 1D well; the energies simply add — the 1D result carries over unchanged.

The same 1/L² law

In a 3D box it is still $E\propto 1/L^2$: the smaller the box, the larger the level spacing. Replace "box" with "nanocrystal" and the physics is identical.

Quantum dots — particle-in-a-box you can see

CdSe quantum dots are semiconductor nanocrystals; the electron is confined in a 3D "box". Changing only the crystal size tunes emission across the whole visible spectrum:

  • Smaller size → smaller box → larger band gap → bluer emission
  • Larger size → smaller band gap → redder emission
2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry

Awarded for the discovery and synthesis of quantum dots — an undergraduate "particle in a box" model grown into a real material in displays and biological markers.

Common misconceptions

Four mistakes worth preventing explicitly

"The wave function is the electron's path."

No. $\psi(x)$ is a probability amplitude. Measurements sample positions according to $|\psi(x)|^2$; the electron is not moving along the drawn sine curve.

"Negative $\psi$ means negative probability."

No. Probability density is $|\psi|^2$, which is never negative. The sign of $\psi$ matters when amplitudes interfere.

"Quantization was assumed."

No. The derivation starts with a continuous differential equation. Discrete energies appear only after applying boundary conditions.

"Infinite wells are real objects."

No. They are idealizations. Real barriers are finite, so wave functions leak into the barrier and energy levels shift.

Summary

Infinite Square Well · Cheat Sheet

Core formulas

$E_n = \dfrac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2} = \dfrac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$
$\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\dfrac{2}{L}}\,\sin\dfrac{n\pi x}{L}$
  • Node rule: $\psi_n$ has $n-1$ nodes
  • Zero-point energy: $E_1 = h^2/8mL^2 \neq 0$
  • Size law: $E_n\propto 1/L^2$, $\;\lambda\propto L^2$
In one sentence

A single boundary condition — "the wave function must vanish at the walls" — simultaneously forces energy quantization, zero-point energy, and wave-function nodes: the three core features of quantum mechanics.

Why it matters for organic electronics

$E_n\propto 1/L^2$ gives the qualitative link: longer conjugation means closer levels, a smaller gap, and redder absorption.
